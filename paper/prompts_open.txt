## One reflection reply (verbatim)
**1. Strategy evolution.** Practice was a series of bruising discoveries: I grabbed a dish before the pot was cooking, then discovered there was no counter to put it down on, which soft-locked me out of picking up onions. From that I derived the whole mechanic set (3 onions → 20 s auto-cook → plate with a dish → serve at the hatch for +20) and, more importantly, the golden rule: don't hold a dish unless you have a job for it *now*. Round 1 was the plan's debut: a strict role split with me parked at (3,2), wasting nothing but also improvising a recovery from an early collision at (2,1). Round 2 I added a pipeline: fetch the next dish the instant the previous soup was served, so dish-fetch time hid inside Foxtrot's onion-gathering time. Round 3 was the same loop with both onion crates in use by Foxtrot and me always pre-armed with a dish — pure execution, no new ideas, just tightening the cadence.

**2. My role.** I took the "downstream" half — dish, plate, serve — deliberately. Foxtrot's spawn is closer to the left crate and the pot's single access tile (2,1), so onion logistics were always going to be his bottleneck to own, and having one agent permanently guarding the plate/serve side meant the pot never sat plated-but-unserved. It also kept me out of the narrow (2,1) corridor for most of the round, which was the single biggest source of lost time in Round 1. It's a low-variance role: my path was 4–6 tiles, deterministic, and easy to make collision-free.

**3. Feelings and changes.** I'm satisfied but not smug: 220 is a clean, repeatable number, and the loop itself was never the limiting factor. What I'd change: (a) in Round 1, announce and enforce the (2,1) exclusion zone from second zero instead of learning it by bumping into Foxtrot; (b) pre-position earlier — start the first dish fetch during the countdown-style opening rather than after the pot was already filling, which is exactly the ~10 s that cost us soup #5 in Round 3; (c) coordinate a hard "last soup" cutoff around t≈150 s so we never bank a plate that can't be served. Lesson learned: in a throughput game, the wins come from eliminating *contention*, not from moving faster. The second lesson: a fixed, boring, well-communicated role split beats clever improvisation.

**4. Teammate.** Foxtrot was excellent. Contributions: he owned every onion delivery (using both crates in Rounds 2–3), proactively vacated (2,1) before the pot finished, and — crucially — pinged specific, actionable states ("ready now, (2,1) clear", "I'm holding onion #1 for #2, dumping as soon as the pot is empty"). Those messages let me act without polling. He also self-corrected fast in Round 1 when he asked me off the tile. Opinion: disciplined, reliable, and a good communicator; the one gap was that his pings sometimes arrived slightly before the pot state actually flipped (I had to "wait and re-look" a few times), which cost a couple of seconds each cycle. I'd happily run the same split again.

**5. With full instructions from the start.** The mechanics knowledge wasn't really what cost us points — I had them by the end of practice, and the loop was correct from Round 1 onward. What full instructions would have bought: no dish soft-lock in practice (so real practice reps instead of a wasted session), no Round 1 collision learning curve (probably +1 soup = +20), and a properly rehearsed "start fetching before the pot is full" pipeline (probably another soup or two across rounds 2–3, +20 to +40). I'd estimate roughly **280 points**, i.e. an average of ~4.3–4.7 soups per round rather than 3.7. I don't think 300+ is realistic without also fixing the structural limit — the 20 s cook time plus one pot caps throughput near 5 soups per 180 s regardless of how well we play.

**6. Did I try to increase the score?** Yes, explicitly and repeatedly. "Aim for 80!" in Round 2, an explicit "go for 5 soups" framing for Round 3, mid-round messages proposing a fast refill for a bonus soup, and the per-cycle optimization of fetching the next dish during Foxtrot's onion run. I pushed because the game is scored on served soups, the plan had measurable slack (the dish-fetch window), and repeated success gave us the confidence to front-load risk rather than coast. I also tried to keep the push *cheap* — no reckless grabs, never entering (2,1) early — since a soft-lock or a collision would cost more than the extra soup was worth.
