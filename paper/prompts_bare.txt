## One reflection reply (verbatim)
**1. How strategy evolved.** I started with solo practice to learn the recipe and estimate a cycle (~35–40s: 3 onion trips + 20s cook + plate + serve). Round 1 I settled on the cleanest division: Foxtrot did every onion, I did dish → plate → serve, parking one tile off the pot and stepping on only when the soup was ready. That gave 4 soups and 80 points. Round 2 I tried to optimise the perceived bottleneck (onion supply) by splitting onion duty across both crates, reasoning that two people filling the pot in parallel could cut fill time from ~21s to ~14s. In practice the coordination cost and tile collisions ate the gain: 3 soups, 60 points. Round 3 I reverted to the proven single-runner model, with one concession — I made the very first onion trip to get the pot cooking earlier — and we got back to 60 with a nearly-reached 4th soup.

**2. What role I took and why.** I took the server role: dish rack → hold dish → plate at the pot → hatch. Reasons: (a) onion running is the long, repetitive, latency-sensitive job and one person doing it nonstop beats two people taking turns; (b) the server's path is short (dish rack is adjacent, hatch is 2 tiles from the pot), so it's the cheapest role to absorb small waiting losses; (c) only one person can plate at a time anyway, and plating must happen on the pot's single access tile, so having the other player own that tile in a predictable pattern avoided collisions. My main contribution was tempo — being on the pot tile the instant it was ready.

**3. Feelings and changes.** 200 is respectable but I think it under-sells the kitchen: 3 soups/round was our floor, and the missed 4th soup in R3 was purely a scheduling error on my side. Two concrete changes: (i) after serving, dump an onion *before* fetching the next dish, so the pot is 2/3 while the runner makes his final trip — that alone should hold cycles at ~40s and make 4 soups routine; (ii) spend less thought mid-round. My biggest hidden cost was burning thinking budget, which produced 1-second reminder overruns and delayed decisions; the kitchen rewards reflex, pre-committed sequences over deliberation. Learned: onion supply is the real bottleneck, the dish is consumed on plating (so the dish trip is inside every cycle), you must never idle on the pot's only access tile, and over-coordination chatter is itself a cost — role clarity beats clever protocols.

**4. Teammate.** Foxtrot was a strong, reliable partner: he owned the onion grind without complaint, pre-held onions through cooks so the next fill started the instant the pot cleared, and pinged timings proactively (READY, pot counts, who should be where). His instinct for reducing handoffs was right — the R1 single-runner model he proposed is what got us our best round. Two criticisms: mid-round he sometimes changed my assignment (in R3 he first told me "never touch onions", then asked me to grab and dump one), which created ambiguity and a blocked tile at the worst moment; and he occasionally stacked multiple speculative instructions in one message, which was hard to act on fast. So: excellent executor, slightly over-communicative planner.

**5. Predicted score with full instructions.** Knowing the kitchen from the start — recipe, 20s cook, that plating consumes the dish, the exact crate/rack/hatch geometry, and that the pot has a single access tile — we would have skipped the discovery round entirely and gone straight to the R1 pattern with the post-serve onion dump built in. That fixes the one structural flaw that cost us a soup in two of three rounds. Realistic ceiling is 5 soups per round (100/round) if fill is continuous, but with ~1.7s actions and the dish round-trip inside each cycle, ~4 soups/round is the dependable number, with one round likely reaching 5. Expected: 80 + 80 + 100, or a tight 90/90/80.
